Master Lesson Prototype

The Great Migration:
Why Did Millions Move?

This sample demonstrates the lesson architecture we can reuse across U.S. History, World History, Black History, Civics & Government, Geography, and World Religions.

Black HistoryInquiry LabGrades 8–12 / Adult45–60 min
1 · Launch

Start With the Question

Essential Question: Why did millions of Black Americans leave the South during the Great Migration, and how did that movement reshape the United States?

Imagine you are deciding whether to leave the place your family has known for generations. You have heard that jobs and greater freedom may exist hundreds of miles away—but moving means uncertainty, expense, and leaving people behind.

Warm-up prompt

What factors might make a person decide that staying is more dangerous or limiting than leaving?

2 · What You'll Learn

Learning Objectives

Explain

Identify major push and pull factors connected to the Great Migration.

Analyze

Use historical evidence to infer why individuals and families made migration decisions.

Connect

Explain how migration changed cities, culture, labor, politics, and American life.

Evaluate

Judge which factors were most important and defend that judgment with evidence.

3 · Build Context

Historical Background

Between roughly 1916 and 1970, millions of Black Americans moved from the rural South to cities in the North, Midwest, and West. They were responding to a combination of pressures and opportunities: racial violence, segregation, limited political rights, low-wage agricultural labor, wartime industrial jobs, expanding Black communities, and hopes for greater personal freedom.

Why historians use “push” and “pull” factors

Push factors are conditions that encourage people to leave a place. Pull factors are conditions that attract people toward a destination. Real migration decisions usually involve both.

4 · Vocabulary

Tap to Reveal Key Terms

The large-scale movement of Black Americans from the South to other regions of the United States during the twentieth century.
Laws, customs, and systems that enforced racial segregation and inequality, especially in the South.
A condition that encourages or pressures people to leave a place.
A condition that attracts people toward another place.
The growth of cities and the movement of people into urban areas.
Evidence created during the period being studied, such as letters, photographs, newspapers, interviews, or government records.
5 · Explore Evidence

Primary Source Analysis

Prototype source image area
Final lessons will use verified public-domain or properly licensed historical images/sources.

Observe → Infer → Question

Observe: What specific details would you record before interpreting the source?
Infer: What might those details suggest about migration, opportunity, or risk?
Question: What else would you need to know before drawing a strong conclusion?
Source-analysis scaffold

Who created the source? When and where was it created? Who was the intended audience? What was happening at the time? What does the source reveal—and what can it not tell us by itself?

6 · Quick Check

Check Your Understanding

Which is the best example of a push factor?
Which is the best example of a pull factor?
7 · Think Like a Historian

Make a Claim

Prompt: Which mattered more in causing the Great Migration: push factors in the South or pull factors in other regions?

Claim-evidence-reasoning reminder

Claim: Answer the question directly. Evidence: Use specific historical information. Reasoning: Explain how your evidence supports the claim.

8 · Apply It

Connect Movement to Change

Migration does more than change where people live. It can transform neighborhoods, labor markets, voting power, music, food, language, business, schools, and political movements.

Application Challenge: Choose one American city affected by the Great Migration. Explain one way migration changed that city's culture, economy, or politics.
9 · Dig Deeper

Extension Options

Option A · Map It

Trace one migration route from a southern state to a northern, midwestern, or western city and explain why that destination may have attracted migrants.

Option B · Compare Voices

Compare two first-person accounts of migration. Identify one shared motivation and one meaningful difference.

Option C · Culture Connection

Investigate how the Great Migration contributed to the Harlem Renaissance, Chicago blues, or another cultural development.

11 · Sources & References

Reference Model

For the final migrated lessons, this area will contain verified sources and rights information. We will favor Library of Congress, National Archives, Smithsonian, government collections, and clearly identified public-domain materials when possible.

  • Library of Congress — Great Migration collections and historical photographs.
  • National Archives — federal records related to migration, labor, and twentieth-century American life.
  • Smithsonian / National Museum of African American History and Culture — contextual resources on Black migration and culture.
