Historical Evidence Packet

These are short educational summaries for practice, not quotations from original historical documents. Ask what each source tells you — and what it leaves out.

Source A · River communities

In parts of the Eastern Woodlands, waterways helped connect communities. Rivers supported travel and exchange. Nearby forests supplied materials for homes and tools.

Instructional geographic summary; not about every nation

Source B · Southwest farming

Some Ancestral Pueblo communities farmed where rain could be limited. People developed ways to collect and manage water. Their methods reflected local knowledge and conditions.

Instructional geographic summary; not about all Southwest peoples

Source C · Museum object

A museum label reads: “Clay vessel. North America. Maker unknown. Date unknown.” The label does not name the nation or explain how the object was used.

Fictional museum-label exercise

Source D · Community knowledge

To learn about Haudenosaunee government, researchers can examine historical records and knowledge shared by Haudenosaunee people. An outsider's account may leave out important perspectives.

Instructional source-evaluation summary

Use different types of evidence

Sources A–D are instructional written summaries. Your written response needs two different kinds of evidence from your lessons or the sources linked here. Do not count two written summaries as two different kinds of evidence.

Landscape and geographic evidence: National Park Service — Indigenous Cultural Landscapes (landscape examples and geographic context).

Archaeological and farming evidence: National Park Service — Ancestral Pueblo Heritage Garden (archaeological, environmental, and farming evidence).

Community-informed historical evidence: Smithsonian NMAI — Haudenosaunee Guide.

Sample response — one way to organize your thinking

In parts of the Eastern Woodlands, rivers helped communities travel, exchange goods, and reach resources. Some Ancestral Pueblo communities in the Southwest developed ways to manage limited water for farming. These practices were different, but each shows how people used knowledge of their homelands.

Source A, a written geographic summary, explains how waterways and forests could help Eastern Woodlands communities. Source B describes water use in some Southwest farming communities. I would also look for a map of rivers and settlements and archaeological evidence of water systems. These different kinds of evidence could help check the written summaries.

One limitation is that the short summaries do not give every nation's perspective or describe every community. They cannot prove that everyone in either region lived in the same way. A stronger investigation would include more specific sources and community knowledge.

Notice: The sample has a comparison, explanations, evidence and a source limitation. The map and archaeology mentioned are proposed additional research, not evidence already examined in the packet. For your assessment, use two actual kinds of evidence from your lessons or research.

Writing Hints: Build Your Historical Comparison

Aim for 2–3 well-developed paragraphs. Explain your ideas clearly, compare two specific societies or regions, and use historical evidence. You do not need a five-paragraph essay for this task.

Paragraph 1 — Introduce and Compare

Name the two Indigenous societies or regions, identify their homelands, and introduce a meaningful similarity or difference.

Paragraph 2 — Explain with Evidence

Explain how each society developed practices suited to its environment. Use at least two kinds of evidence, such as artifacts, maps, oral histories, archaeology, or written accounts. Explain how that evidence supports your comparison.

Paragraph 3 — Evaluate and Conclude (recommended)

Explain one limitation of your evidence or a missing perspective. End with a careful conclusion. Avoid generalizing about all Indigenous nations. Two strong paragraphs are also acceptable if they cover every part of the task.

Before you submit: Did you compare two societies, explain each practice, use two kinds of evidence, discuss a limitation, and avoid overgeneralizations?

Historian's Challenge · Create and Share (Optional)

Mission: You are a museum curator. Someone wants your museum to say that all Indigenous nations lived alike. Show why that statement is not accurate.

Make an exhibit card, two-slide presentation, illustrated comparison, or short narrated explanation. Choose two specific societies or regions. Identify a practice in each, use two kinds of evidence, explain what one source cannot tell you, and ask a question you would investigate next.

Reading the score

The first four formats provide 15 automatically checked points. Writing contributes up to 12 self-reviewed or teacher-reviewed rubric points, not an automatically judged writing grade. A score of at least 22/27 reaches 80%; the overall result is available when every response and rubric row is complete.

Standards: thematic alignment with Maryland social studies Standards 2, 3, 5 and 6. Specific district indicator alignment should be confirmed before formal use. Not an official Maryland or psychometrically validated test.

Adjustments: read questions aloud, allow additional time, provide a print transcript or score the written response orally using the same rubric. Maintain the outcome, not one mandated response medium.

Reusable Grades 5–8 assessment blueprint

Preserve approved color, headings, navigation, scoring, and rubric mechanics. Adapt source lengths, demands, question types, and writing expectations by grade.
