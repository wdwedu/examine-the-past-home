Essential Questions

Main inquiry: How did different Indigenous nations use their environments, knowledge, and forms of government to shape North America before European colonization?

  1. How did communities in different regions use their homelands in distinctive ways?
  2. How can maps, objects, and knowledge shared by Native nations help us explain the past?
  3. Why can one image or example never represent every Indigenous nation?

These lesson questions support Unit 5.1’s inquiry into Indigenous North America and early encounters.

Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Explain one relationship between environment and community practices in each of two regional examples, using a relevant detail from each reading.
  2. Compare two kinds of historical sources, identifying what each helps explain and one question each leaves unanswered.
  3. Construct a historical explanation in writing or a presentation, using at least two relevant details from different types of sources and explaining how the evidence supports the claim.

Success Criteria

  • Accuracy: name the regions or communities being discussed and avoid treating all Native nations as one culture.
  • Evidence: identify the sources of at least two relevant details.
  • Reasoning: explain the environment–practice connection and how the evidence supports the claim; acknowledge one limitation or unanswered question.
  • Communication: organize the explanation clearly and use respectful, precise vocabulary.

The evidence checkpoint, source comparison, final explanation, and individual exit ticket show progress toward these objectives. Independent and group work use the same success criteria.

Introduction

Quick pre-check

Which statement is best supported by historians and Native communities?
Vocabulary · Open before reading

Indigenous: originating in and connected to a place over generations. Nation: a people with shared political and cultural identity. Homeland: a place to which a community has long-standing relationships and responsibilities. Evidence: information used to support a historical explanation. Oral history: knowledge passed down through spoken accounts. Governance: how communities organize decisions and responsibilities. Adaptation: changing practices in response to circumstances.

Say it: Choose any two words, explain them in your own terms, and use one in a sentence.

Launch prompt: Imagine you are asked to describe every community in North America using just one photograph. What information might you miss?

Historical Evidence Lab · Build a responsible explanation

Historians do not assume that every nation had the same government, buildings, farming methods, or traditions. These three cards are evidence-category examples written for this lesson, not quotations or reproductions of primary sources. Next, consult a real museum source and record its origin, context and limitations.

Model the historian’s thinking

Observe: What does the source actually show or say? Interpret: What can that detail help explain? Question: What would we need another source to confirm?

Work through Evidence A together, then compare it with Evidence B or C. Use the museum guide to test your explanation against a specific source.

Evidence A · Environment

Maps show different river systems, coastlines, deserts and grasslands. People developed locally appropriate ways to grow, gather, hunt, fish, travel and exchange resources.

Ask: What can a map show? What can't a map tell you about laws or language?

Evidence B · Material culture

Homes, tools, agricultural features and other objects can reveal technologies and relationships with place. An object alone cannot tell us everything about its maker.

Ask: What evidence would help interpret an object without guessing?

Evidence C · Native voices

Tribal community knowledge, oral histories and nation-created resources provide essential perspectives for interpreting the past and understanding living communities today.

Ask: Who is explaining this history, and whose voice is missing?

Source-study extension · Native perspectives and artifacts

04 · Apply the learning · Writing or presentation

Independent / Group Practice

Choose how to work. Every pathway answers the same inquiry and uses the same success criteria.

How did different Indigenous nations shape North America before European colonization? Compare two regional examples and use historical evidence to support your explanation.

Historical explanation · Shared organizer

1. Claim: State a specific, defensible idea and name the regional examples or communities being compared. 2. Evidence: Use at least two details from different types of sources, such as a reading and a museum map or object. Name each source. 3. Reasoning: Explain how each detail supports your claim and name a question still worth investigating.

Success Criteria and Teacher Scoring Guide · 0–3 points per dimension

Claim: accurate, appropriately specific, avoids treating Indigenous peoples as a single culture. Evidence: at least two relevant details with sources identified. Reasoning: explains the relationship between evidence and claim, notes limits. Communication: clear, respectful, organized explanation.

0 = absent; 1 = emerging; 2 = proficient; 3 = strong. Suggested mastery: 9/12 with no dimension below 2. Use the same rubric for a written response or a presentation. Assess each student’s understanding using their individual exit ticket as well as the shared product.

06 · Return to the inquiry · Reflect individually

Closure

How has the evidence changed your answer to the essential question?

Individual Exit Ticket

Whether you worked independently, with a partner, or in a group, answer these questions yourself:

  1. Compare how two regional examples used their environments. Support each example with a detail from the reading.
  2. Choose two kinds of sources from the lesson. What did each help you explain, and what question did each leave unanswered?
  3. Correct one generalization about Indigenous North America, using evidence from your work.
Teacher Reflection

Review each student’s explanation and exit ticket against the three objectives. Which learners can make an accurate comparison, explain the sources, and support a claim? Which need another modeled example or a different source? Use those observations to plan the next lesson.

07 · Optional enrichment · Accessible alternatives

Extensions

Optional homework · 15–25 minutes

Choose one Indigenous nation, ideally using a source created with or by that community. Record its name, homeland, one historical practice or governance structure, and one way the community continues today. Cite at least one source and explain why the source is trustworthy. Alternative: create a two-minute audio explanation rather than a paragraph.

Three field-trip possibilities · DMV first
  1. Washington, DC — Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian. Examine maps or cultural objects and ask who made them, how they're interpreted, and how they connect to living nations. After the visit: make a two-evidence claim. Official visit details ↗
  2. Phoenix, Arizona — Heard Museum. Compare material culture and relationships with homelands of Southwest Native communities; create an annotated comparison chart. School visits have eligibility, booking and seasonal constraints. Official school visits ↗
  3. Oklahoma City, Oklahoma — First Americans Museum. Examine stories of Oklahoma's Tribal Nations, select an object with cultural context, and reflect on continuity into the present. Official field trips ↗

Virtual alternative: select two resources from Smithsonian Native Knowledge 360°, compare what each makes visible, and write one respectful source question. Check current museum policies before planning a visit.
