North America Before European Colonization

Before European colonization, many different Indigenous nations built communities, made decisions, traded, developed technologies, and cared for their homelands. How can evidence help us see this complex history?

01 · Backward design · Teacher planning and learner goals

What will you learn?

Essential question: How did different Indigenous nations use their environments, knowledge, and forms of government to shape North America before European colonization?

Learning outcomes · I can…
  1. Identify at least three ways Indigenous nations adapted to different environments.
  2. Compare two regional communities without treating all Native peoples as one culture.
  3. Evaluate at least two pieces of historical evidence and explain what each can and cannot tell us.
  4. Construct a short claim supported by evidence about life in North America before colonization.

Observable outcomes align the pre-check, inquiry, exit ticket, and unit assessment. Bloom's progression: remember → understand → analyze → evaluate → create.

Standards and disciplinary literacy

Maryland Social Studies: thematic alignment to Standards 2 (Peoples of the Nations and World), 3 (Geography), 5 (History), and 6 (Skills and Processes). Grade-specific benchmark codes require verification against the local district scope and sequence; none are invented here.

ELA connections: evidence-based explanations, comparing texts and visual sources, academic vocabulary, and collaborative discussion. Teachers should match local grade-level Common Core codes to their adopted texts.

Maryland Department of Education — official frameworks

Teacher delivery · 45 / 60 / 90 minutes

45 min: 5-min pre-check; 8-min branching reading; 6-min video or transcript; 12-min evidence lab; 9-min claim; 5-min exit ticket.

60 min: include vocabulary, discussion and two paths in the reading.

90 min: compare all three regional pathways, investigate additional sources, conduct a 15-minute group evidence council, peer-review claims, and allow time for revision.

Substitute-ready: Project the question; select Group; read the on-screen directions aloud; pause at each checkpoint; collect claim + exit ticket. No account or student identifying information is needed.

Instructional design rationale · ADDIE and UDL

Analyze: start with misconceptions about a single Indigenous culture and assess prior knowledge. Design: align each activity with observable outcomes. Develop: use parallel text/video, retrieval checks, and a branching inquiry. Implement: offer independent, partners, group, projection and accessible transcript options. Evaluate: use question feedback, a written performance task, and the auto-scored check to revise instruction.

Accessibility: Georgia text, high contrast, keyboard-operable buttons, captions when supplied by the video host, text alternatives and no mandatory timers. Do not treat one video or reading as a universal learning preference.

02 · Engage · Diagnose · Prepare

Before we begin

Quick pre-check

Which statement is best supported by historians and Native communities?
Vocabulary · Open before reading

Indigenous: originating in and connected to a place over generations. Nation: a people with shared political and cultural identity. Homeland: a place to which a community has long-standing relationships and responsibilities. Evidence: information used to support a historical explanation. Oral history: knowledge passed down through spoken accounts. Governance: how communities organize decisions and responsibilities. Adaptation: changing practices in response to circumstances.

Say it: Choose any two words, explain them in your own terms, and use one in a sentence.

Launch prompt: Imagine you are asked to describe every community in North America using just one photograph. What information might you miss?

04 · Analyze · Historical evidence lab

Build a responsible explanation

Historians do not assume that every nation had the same government, buildings, farming methods, or traditions. Choose a teaching mode; the activity instructions update without changing the historical question.

Evidence A · Environment

Maps show different river systems, coastlines, deserts and grasslands. People developed locally appropriate ways to grow, gather, hunt, fish, travel and exchange resources.

Ask: What can a map show? What can't a map tell you about laws or language?

Evidence B · Material culture

Homes, tools, agricultural features and other objects can reveal technologies and relationships with place. An object alone cannot tell us everything about its maker.

Ask: What evidence would help interpret an object without guessing?

Evidence C · Native voices

Tribal community knowledge, oral histories and nation-created resources provide essential perspectives for interpreting the past and understanding living communities today.

Ask: Who is explaining this history, and whose voice is missing?

05 · Create · Performance task

Make a claim, support it

Respond to the essential question: How did Indigenous nations shape the continent before European colonization?

Historical argument · 3-part organizer

1. Claim: State a defensible idea (not a generalization). 2. Evidence: Use two details from different types of sources. 3. Reasoning: Explain how each detail supports your claim and name a question still worth investigating.

Teacher scoring guide · 0–3 points per dimension

Claim: accurate, appropriately specific, avoids treating Indigenous peoples as a single culture. Evidence: at least two relevant details with sources identified. Reasoning: explains the relationship between evidence and claim, notes limits. Communication: clear, respectful, organized explanation.

0 = absent; 1 = emerging; 2 = proficient; 3 = strong. Suggested mastery: 9/12 with no dimension below 2. This is a locally designed rubric, not a published validated instrument.

07 · Optional enrichment · Accessible alternatives

Extend the investigation

Optional homework · 15–25 minutes

Choose one Indigenous nation, ideally using a source created with or by that community. Record its name, homeland, one historical practice or governance structure, and one way the community continues today. Cite at least one source and explain why the source is trustworthy. Alternative: create a two-minute audio explanation rather than a paragraph.

Three field-trip possibilities · DMV first
  1. Washington, DC — Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian. Examine maps or cultural objects and ask who made them, how they're interpreted, and how they connect to living nations. After the visit: make a two-evidence claim. Official visit details ↗
  2. Phoenix, Arizona — Heard Museum. Compare material culture and relationships with homelands of Southwest Native communities; create an annotated comparison chart. School visits have eligibility, booking and seasonal constraints. Official school visits ↗
  3. Oklahoma City, Oklahoma — First Americans Museum. Examine stories of Oklahoma's Tribal Nations, select an object with cultural context, and reflect on continuity into the present. Official field trips ↗

Virtual alternative: select two resources from Smithsonian Native Knowledge 360°, compare what each makes visible, and write one respectful source question. Check current museum policies before planning a visit.

08 · Evaluation · Evidence and transparent design

Why these strategies? Where did the facts come from?

Instructional strategy and effectiveness notes

The lesson uses backward design to map outcomes to a claim and short assessment; retrieval practice through low-stakes knowledge checks; scaffolding through vocabulary and branch-by-branch reading; and formative feedback through explanation-rich scoring. Multiple means of representation and expression offer access without assuming fixed “learning styles.”

Evidence and caution: these approaches have substantial educational research foundations, but this specific lesson has not been evaluated with learners. No completion-rate, engagement, test-score, or learning-gain statistics are claimed. A teacher or portfolio reviewer can examine the alignment, tasks, rubric, accessibility and test results to judge the design.
